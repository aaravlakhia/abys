Side A · The permanent collection

Every artist on this labelis already gone.

These recordings exist. What they recorded does not. There is no remaster coming, no reunion tour, no second take — the master tape is the entire remaining evidence that the sound was ever made on Earth. Every track below is synthesised live in your browser from what the originals are described to sound like.

REQ‑001
Aves · bird Extinct 2023

Kauaʻi ʻōʻō

Alakaʻi plateau, Kauaʻi, Hawaiʻi

The male half of a mating duet. He sings his part, then leaves a gap for the female's answer. The gap is the reason this recording is unbearable — there was no female left to fill it. He was the last of his species, singing to an empty forest.

A looping reconstruction. Each cycle plays his two-note phrase, then roughly three and a half seconds of forest where the female's answering phrase belongs. No answer ever arrives.

REQ‑002

Ivory‑billed woodpecker

Singer Tract, Louisiana

A 1935 expedition hauled a tonne of recording equipment into old‑growth swamp and captured its double knock — two hammer blows on a trunk, a tenth of a second apart — and its flat, nasal call. The forest was logged soon after.

A looping reconstruction of the double knock and the nasal call, over swamp ambience.

Central Panama

A chytrid fungus swept Panama faster than anyone could collect survivors. The last known individual, a male kept in Atlanta and nicknamed Toughie, called for years into a room with no other frog in it. He died in 2016 and took the species with him.

REQ‑006
Insecta · invertebrate Collapsing

The flying insects

Krefeld reserves, Germany

No single species — the layer underneath all the others. Volunteers ran the same malaise traps in the same nature reserves for twenty‑seven years and weighed what they caught. Three quarters of the biomass simply stopped arriving. This is the sound that used to fill a summer field.

A looping reconstruction of a summer field chorus: cricket stridulation and cicada drone.

Synthesised reconstruction of a field chorus. Hallmann et al. (2017) measured a decline of more than 75% in flying insect biomass over 27 years.

The part nobody expects

Here is the strangest thingwe have learned: playback works.

On dead patches of coral reef, researchers rigged underwater speakers and played the sound of a healthy reef — the crackle, the grunts, the clicks. Fish arrived. Roughly twice as many as on the silent patches, and they stayed. The recording did not just document the reef. It started rebuilding it.

That is the whole argument of this page. A recording is not only a headstone. Kept early enough, and played back loud enough, it is a set of instructions for putting something back. Every soundscape still being captured right now is a rescue plan we do not yet know how to use.

The ʻōʻō recording came too late to save anything. The next one doesn't have to.

What actually moves the needle

Three things. Pick one today.

01

Record your own dawn

Open a sound‑monitoring app, put your phone on a windowsill at first light, and upload it. Acoustic citizen science is how the decline above was measured in the first place — the dataset is built from ordinary mornings.

02

Make your street survivable

Window decals to stop collisions. Cats kept in at dawn and dusk. Native plants instead of lawn. Skip the pesticide. These are the interventions with the highest measured effect per person — unglamorous, and they work.

03

Fund the microphones

Sound archives and field recordists are running a race against silence on almost no money. Backing a bioacoustics archive or a habitat trust buys the one thing that can't be bought later: the recording made in time.
