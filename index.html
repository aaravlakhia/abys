Side A · The permanent collection

Every artist on this label
is already gone.

These recordings exist. What they recorded does not. There is no remaster coming, no reunion tour, no second take — the master tape is the entire remaining evidence that the sound was ever made on Earth.

REQ‑001

Kauaʻi ʻōʻō

Alakaʻi plateau, Kauaʻi, Hawaiʻi

The male half of a mating duet. He sings his part, then leaves a gap for the female's answer. The gap is the reason this recording is unbearable — there was no female left to fill it. He was the last of his species, singing to an empty forest.

Recorded 1987 Extinct 2023
REQ‑002

Ivory‑billed woodpecker

Singer Tract, Louisiana

A 1935 expedition hauled a tonne of recording equipment into old‑growth swamp and captured its call. The forest was logged soon after. Those few minutes of audio are, to this day, the closest anyone can get to the bird.

Recorded 1935 Last seen 1944
REQ‑003

Okjökull

Borgarfjörður, west Iceland

Not an animal — a glacier. Meltwater, shifting ice, the low structural groan of a body of ice big enough to have a name. In 2014 it stopped being a glacier. A plaque was bolted to the bare rock in 2019, addressed to whoever comes next.

Named since 1890s Declared dead 2014

The volume knob is turning left

This is not a handful of rare birds.
It is the background noise of the planet.

2.9 billion

breeding birds lost from the United States and Canada since 1970 — roughly one in four, gone in a single human lifetime.

25 years

of recordings across North America and Europe show dawn choruses growing measurably quieter and less varied. The spring you remember was louder. That is not nostalgia — it is data.

Side B · The part nobody expects

Here is the strangest thing
we have learned: playback works.

On dead patches of coral reef, researchers rigged underwater speakers and played the sound of a healthy reef — the crackle, the grunts, the clicks. Fish arrived. Twice as many as on the silent patches, and they stayed. The recording did not just document the reef. It started rebuilding it.

That is the whole argument of this page. A recording is not only a headstone. Kept early enough, and played back loud enough, it is a set of instructions for putting something back. Every soundscape still being captured right now is a rescue plan we do not yet know how to use.

The ʻōʻō recording came too late to save anything. The next one doesn't have to.

What actually moves the needle

Three things. Pick one today.

01

Record your own dawn

Open a sound‑monitoring app, put your phone on a windowsill at first light, and upload it. Acoustic citizen science is how the 25‑year decline was measured in the first place — the dataset is built from ordinary mornings.

02

Make your street survivable

Window decals to stop collisions. Cats kept in at dawn and dusk. Native plants instead of lawn. Skip the pesticide. These are the interventions with the highest measured effect per person — unglamorous, and they work.

03

Fund the microphones

Sound archives and field recordists are running a race against silence on almost no money. Backing a bioacoustics archive or a habitat trust buys the one thing that can't be bought later: the recording made in time.
